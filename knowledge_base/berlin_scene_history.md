# Berlin Electronic Music Scene — History

## The Wall falls and a city opens (1989–1991)

The fall of the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989 is the founding event of Berlin's club scene. Overnight, a city divided for 28 years had access to thousands of abandoned buildings — warehouses, department stores, power plants, bunkers, underpasses — in the formerly no-man's land between East and West. The GDR's infrastructure was empty and unclaimed, the police were overwhelmed, and a generation of young people from both sides of the city had an entire urban landscape to colonise.

The first parties happened immediately: in derelict buildings in Mitte and Prenzlauer Berg, often without electricity (generators), heated only by body heat. The sound system was whatever could be transported in a van. The music was the electronic dance music arriving from Detroit and Chicago via London and Frankfurt — techno and house on cassette tapes and vinyl.

**Tresor** opened in March 1991 in the vaults of the Wertheim department store on Potsdamer Platz — a site that had been in the death strip for decades. It was the world's first major techno venue with a deliberately brutal aesthetic: low ceilings, no lighting, fog machines, and a direct connection to the Detroit techno scene. Jeff Mills, Robert Hood, Blake Baxter, and Juan Atkins played early. The vault — known as "the Globus" — became the template for techno club design worldwide.

## The 1990s: the golden era of the Love Parade and warehouse raves

Throughout the 1990s, Berlin's scene expanded rapidly. The **Love Parade** — a techno street parade that began in 1989 with 150 people and grew to over a million by the late 1990s — made Berlin's electronic music culture globally visible. Clubs like **E-Werk** (a former power station), **Planet** (Prenzlauer Berg), and dozens of temporary locations hosted all-night and all-weekend events.

The city's legal ambiguity was crucial: reunification left property ownership unclear for years, giving squatters and club organisers time before developers arrived. The German concept of "Grauzone" (grey zone) — spaces that existed outside normal commercial and legal frameworks — defined the era.

**BPitch Control** was founded by Ellen Allien in 1999, becoming one of Berlin's most important labels and helping establish the city's specific sound: harder than UK house, more melodic and club-oriented than pure Detroit techno. Paul Kalkbrenner, Sascha Funke, and Modeselektor all emerged from the BPitch orbit.

## The 2000s: Berghain and Berlin's global dominance

**Berghain** opened in 2004 in a former heating plant in Friedrichshain, near the former Berlin Wall border. It was founded by Norbert Thormann and Michael Teufele, with Ostgut Ton as its record label. The club had two floors: the main floor (Berghain) for hard, long-form techno, and Panorama Bar upstairs for house and more melodic sounds. Ben Klock and Marcel Dettmann became its resident DJs and the faces of Berlin techno globally.

Berghain's combination of extraordinary acoustic design (20-metre ceilings, 60-kilowatt sound system), strict and non-discriminatory door policy (protecting queer and kinky communities), no-phone policy, and marathon opening hours (Friday midnight to Monday morning, continuously) made it the most discussed and imitated club in the world.

**Ostgut Ton**, Berghain's in-house label, became one of the most respected techno labels of the 2000s and 2010s, releasing Shed, Fiedel, Steffi, and Virginia alongside Klock and Dettmann.

**Watergate** opened in 2002 on the Spree in Kreuzberg, focusing on a different sound: deeper, more melodic, more house-oriented. Dixon, Âme, and the Innervisions label became its defining voice.

## Key venues (active)

- **Berghain / Panorama Bar** — Friedrichshain. The reference point. Techno / house.
- **Tresor** — now in a former power plant in Mitte. Still the Detroit-Berlin axis.
- **Watergate** — Kreuzberg, on the Spree. Melodic house and techno.
- **//about blank** — Friedrichshain. Queer, political, diverse programming.
- **Sisyphos** — Rummelsburg. Outdoor garden, house and disco-influenced.
- **OHM** — beneath Tresor. More experimental, bass music, UK sounds.
- **Klunkerkranich** — Neukölln, rooftop. Relaxed, daytime/evening techno and house.
- **Ritter Butzke** — Kreuzberg. Mid-size, eclectic programming.
- **Club der Visionaere (CDV)** — Treptow, canal-side. Minimal and deep house.

## Why Berlin stayed on top

Most cities that had a golden era of club culture lost it to development, legislation, or gentrification. Berlin has been unusually resilient because:

1. The city government has, since the 2000s, officially recognised club culture as part of Berlin's cultural infrastructure — clubs can apply for cultural venue status, reducing noise-complaint exposure.
2. Cheap rents (historically) allowed clubs to survive on door revenue rather than drinks income.
3. The **Clubcommission Berlin** (founded 2001) is a trade body that lobbies city government on behalf of clubs — a formal structure that doesn't exist in most cities.
4. The city's identity is bound up with its nightlife. "Poor but sexy" (Wowereit's 2003 phrase) captured a generation; the creative class attracted by cheap Berlin rents in the 2000s became the audience and workforce for the scene.

## The threat: gentrification and COVID

Dozens of iconic venues have closed due to rising rents and residential development: E-Werk (1997), Ostbahnhof venues, and many others. COVID-19 closed all clubs from March 2020 to summer 2022, threatening the economic survival of the scene. Most major venues survived due to government support and community fundraising, but the landscape changed — smaller venues and promoters were hit hardest.

The tension between Berlin's club culture and its rapid gentrification (international capital, rising rents in Mitte, Prenzlauer Berg, and now Friedrichshain and Neukölln) is the defining challenge of the current decade.
