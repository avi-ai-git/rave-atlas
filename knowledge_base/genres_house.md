# House

## What it is

House music was born in Chicago in the early 1980s in a club called the Warehouse — hence the name — where DJ Frankie Knuckles played a blend of disco, soul, and electronic music for a predominantly Black and gay audience that had been pushed out of mainstream nightlife. The music that emerged was built on drum machines (the Roland TR-808 and TR-909), synthesisers, and soulful vocals or piano riffs. It was music made for and by people who needed a space of their own.

Where techno was mechanical and futurist, house was warm, soulful, and grounded in the African-American musical tradition of gospel, funk, and disco. The tension and interplay between house and techno defined the evolution of club music through the 1990s and 2000s, and still does today.

## BPM and rhythmic signature

- **BPM range:** 118–128 BPM is classic house. Deep house sits at the lower end (118–122); Chicago house and acid house around 122–128; tech house and harder variants push to 128–132.
- **Kick pattern:** Four-on-the-floor, like techno — but the feel is entirely different. House kicks are rounder, warmer, less compressed. They bounce rather than pound.
- **Hi-hat:** The signature of house is the open hi-hat on the offbeat (the "and" between beats) — this is what gives house its characteristic swing and forward motion. Listen for the "tss-tss-tss-tss" shuffle.
- **Clap/snare:** Claps typically on beat 2 and 4, often layered and fat. The clap in house is meant to make you move; in techno it's more of a texture.
- **Groove character:** House breathes. Compared to techno's relentless compression, house has an organic quality — it swings, it sways, it makes you want to move your whole body rather than just your feet.

## How to recognise it

- The offbeat open hi-hat is the clearest marker. If you hear that shuffle between the kicks, it's in house territory.
- Warmth: house uses warm pads, organ stabs, piano riffs, and soulful vocals. The palate is human and emotional where techno is industrial and abstract.
- Bass in house is prominent and melodic — often a round, warm sub that you feel more than hear. Funky bass lines are common, especially in Chicago and deep house.
- Vocals: house is far more likely to have full or chopped vocal lines. Diva vocals, gospel-influenced phrases, looped speech samples.
- The feeling of joy or release — house music was built in spaces where people could be free. That emotional dimension is built into the DNA.

## Defining subgenres

- **Deep house:** Slow, warm, introspective. Larry Heard (Mr. Fingers), Larry Heard's "Can You Feel It" is the reference point. Chords, atmosphere, late-night melancholy.
- **Chicago house:** The original. Roland TR-909 kick, TB-303 bass, claps, piano stabs. Frankie Knuckles, Larry Heard, Ron Hardy.
- **UK garage / UKG:** 2-step rhythm, chopped vocals, pitched-up samples. London, late 1990s. Craig David era.
- **Afro house / Afrobeats-influenced house:** Percussive, tribal, spiritual. South African producers. Black Coffee, Da Capo, Enoo Napa. Big in Berlin.
- **Tech house:** The intersection of techno's functionality and house's groove. Driving and punchy, less emotional than deep house. Jamie Jones, Green Velvet, Detlef.
- **Lo-fi house:** Deliberately degraded audio — vinyl crackle, tape saturation, bitcrushing. Nostalgia and texture over clarity.
- **Melodic house / melodic techno:** European, more recent. Progressive chord structures and melodies layered over house or techno rhythms. Âme, Dixon, Tale Of Us. Very popular in Berlin's Watergate and Bar25 circuit.

## Key artists

- **Frankie Knuckles** — the Godfather of House. Created the genre at the Warehouse.
- **Larry Heard (Mr. Fingers)** — invented deep house. "Can You Feel It" (1986) is the genre's founding document.
- **Ron Hardy** — Chicago. Wilder and more experimental than Knuckles.
- **Larry Levan** — New York's Paradise Garage. Parallel evolution to Chicago; more soulful and eclectic.
- **Dixon** — Berlin-based. Ostgut Ton / Innervisions. Long, emotive DJ sets; the face of Berlin melodic house.
- **Âme** — Berlin duo (Frank Wiedemann + Kristian Beyer). Innervisions label. Elegant, emotional, European deep house.
- **Black Coffee** — South African. Afro house that crossed into global mainstream. Resident at Hï Ibiza.
- **Jamie Jones** — Welsh, London-based. Tech house. Hot Creations label.

## Key labels (Berlin / relevant to Berlin)

- **Innervisions** — Dixon and Âme's label. The sound of Berlin's more melodic, emotional house scene. Watergate and Bar25 crowd.
- **Ostgut Ton** — yes, also house: Nick Höppner, Cassy, Sonja Moonear.
- **BPitch Control** — Ellen Allien's Berlin label. Eclectic — house, techno, electro. Sascha Funke, Paul Kalkbrenner, Modeselektor.
- **Perlon** — Minimal house and techno. Ricardo Villalobos, Zip, Markus Nicolai. Understated and influential.
- **Hot Creations** — Jamie Jones's London label. Tech house standard-bearer.

## How a house track is structured

House structure is more flexible than techno but still DJ-functional:

1. **Intro (1–2 min):** Rhythm and bass only, or a single repeated element. Shorter intros than techno — house DJs mix faster.
2. **Verse / first drop (1–2 min):** Main groove established. Bass line in, chords start.
3. **Breakdown (30s–1 min):** Common in house. Drums fall away, melody or vocal is exposed. Emotional moment.
4. **Drop / chorus (1–2 min):** Everything returns, often with a new element (vocal hook, piano stab). The peak moment.
5. **Outro (1–2 min):** Strips back to rhythm for mixing out.

House tracks are generally shorter than techno tracks (5–7 min vs 8–10 min) because house DJs typically mix more frequently and the music has more structure to work with.

## Berlin scene context

Berlin's house scene lives parallel to its techno scene and often overlaps. The key venues are different: **Watergate** (Kreuzberg, on the Spree) and **Bar25 / Holzmarkt** for deeper, more melodic sounds; **//about blank** for queer house and techno crossover; **Sisyphos** for long-form house and disco. The Innervisions label and Dixon's legendary Panorama Bar sets at Berghain (Berghain's upper floor is softer and more house-oriented than the main floor) helped establish Berlin as a hub for European deep house alongside its techno reputation.

Afro house has grown significantly in Berlin — Black Coffee's residency influence, combined with a large South African and West African diaspora, has made it a regular fixture in the city's club programme. If you hear percussion-heavy, spiritual-feeling house in Berlin, it's probably in this family.
