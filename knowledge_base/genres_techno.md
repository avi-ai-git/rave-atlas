# Techno

## What it is

Techno is a form of electronic dance music that emerged in Detroit in the mid-1980s, born from the collision of African-American musical traditions, funk, soul, electro, with the industrial decay of a post-automotive city and the futurism of Kraftwerk. It crossed the Atlantic in the late 1980s, took root in Berlin after the Wall fell in 1989, and became the defining soundtrack of a city rebuilding itself in empty warehouses and bunkers.

Where Detroit techno was sparse and cosmic, Berlin techno turned harder, darker, and more hypnotic. The city's all-night (and all-weekend) club culture, anchored by Tresor from 1991 and Berghain from 2004, shaped a style of music built for long-duration dancing in unlit rooms.

## BPM and rhythmic signature

- **BPM range:** 130-150 BPM is the Berlin norm; peak-time and industrial techno can reach 155-160; ambient and dub techno sits lower at 120-130.
- **Kick pattern:** A driving, relentless four-on-the-floor kick, every beat, no gaps. This is the heartbeat of techno; everything else orbits it.
- **Hi-hat:** Open and closed hi-hats create forward momentum; shuffled hats add swing. The "train" hi-hat pattern (rapid 16th-note hats) is a classic techno signature.
- **Clap/snare:** Typically on beat 2 and 4, often processed until metallic and barely recognisable. Snares are more of a texture than a marker.
- **Rhythm character:** Repetitive and hypnotic by design. Unlike house, which breathes and swings, techno compresses, it locks you into a trance state through relentless repetition with micro-variations rather than melodic development.

## How to recognise it

- The kick is always there. If the kick drops out completely for more than a bar, it's probably not peak-time techno.
- Atmosphere over melody: techno builds tension through texture (filters sweeping, synth stabs emerging and retreating) rather than chord progressions or memorable hooks.
- Industrial DNA: the sounds are often metallic, dystopian, machine-like. Think factory floors and rail yards, not summer festivals.
- Long intros and outros for mixing: tracks are often 8-10 minutes, with 2-3 minutes of intro specifically designed for beatmix transitions.
- Minimalism: less is more. Subgenres like minimal techno take this to an extreme, the whole track might be a kick, a hi-hat, a filtered bass loop, and a single synth motif.

## Defining subgenres

- **Detroit techno:** The origin. Juan Atkins, Derrick May, Kevin Saunderson (the Belleville Three). Cosmic, melodic, emotional beneath the machine exterior. Model 500, Rhythim Is Rhythim.
- **Berlin techno:** Hard, dark, long. Berghain sound. Ben Klock, Marcel Dettmann, Surgeon, Blawan.
- **Dub techno:** Slow, cavernous, reverb-drenched. Basic Channel (Mark Ernestus + Moritz von Oswald) invented it. Feels like techno underwater. Buried bass, ghost chords, infinite decay.
- **Industrial techno:** Aggressive, confrontational. Distorted kicks, noise, BPMs above 145. Paula Temple, Rebekah, Ancient Methods.
- **Minimal techno:** Stripped to its skeleton. Popularised by Ricardo Villalobos, Richie Hawtin. Hypnotic through subtraction rather than addition.
- **Acid techno:** The Roland TB-303 bassline is the star, squelching, resonant, unstable. Shared DNA with acid house. Chris Liberator, Surgeon.

## Key artists (Berlin / Berlin-adjacent)

- **Ben Klock**, Berghain resident. Long, dark, hypnotic sets. Label: Klockworks.
- **Marcel Dettmann**, Berghain resident. Gritty, functional, no-frills techno. Label: MDR.
- **Surgeon**, Birmingham but deeply Berlin in spirit. Brutalist sound design.
- **Blawan**, Industrial, percussive, physically overwhelming. Ternesc label.
- **Paula Temple**, Noise and industrial techno. Noise Manifesto.
- **Objekt**, Berlin-based, percussive and unpredictable. TJ Hertz.
- **Dj Stingray**, Detroit → world. Electro and techno boundary.
- **Ancient Methods**, Berlin. Hard industrial techno. One of the city's heaviest acts.

## Key labels (Berlin)

- **Ostgut Ton**, Berghain's label. Ben Klock, Marcel Dettmann, Shed. The house sound of the world's most famous club.
- **Tresor Records**, Born with the club in 1991. Detroit-Berlin axis. Surgeon, Jeff Mills, Robert Hood on the Detroit side; Planetary Assault Systems, Truncate on the Berlin side.
- **Klockworks**, Ben Klock's label. Dark, functional, no decoration.
- **MDR**, Marcel Dettmann Records. Straight, hard, honest techno.
- **Ternesc**, Blawan's label. Industrial edge.
- **Stroboscopic Artefacts**, Berlin. Lucy, Dadub. More experimental and cerebral end of techno.

## How a techno track is structured (for DJs)

A typical Berlin techno track has:
1. **Intro (2-3 min):** Kick + hi-hats only, or kick + sparse percussion. Designed for beatmixing in over another track.
2. **Build (1-2 min):** Elements enter, bass, synth texture, effects. Energy rises.
3. **Main body (3-5 min):** Full groove. All elements present. The "peak" of the track.
4. **Breakdown (optional, 30s, 1 min):** Kick drops out, atmosphere swells. Creates tension before the re-drop.
5. **Outro (2-3 min):** Elements drop away, leaving kick + hi-hat again. Designed for mixing out.

This structure is functional architecture, not pop songwriting. The track exists to serve the DJ and the dancefloor, not to be memorable outside the club.

## Berlin scene context

Techno arrived in Berlin via the Love Parade (from 1989) and clubs like Tresor (1991, Mitte), E-Werk (1993), and later Berghain (2004, Friedrichshain). The fall of the Wall left a city full of empty industrial spaces, power plants, department stores, bunkers, that became the world's most important techno venues. This geography shaped the sound: the music had to fill vast, acoustically brutal spaces, which pushed DJs and producers toward long, hypnotic, bass-heavy tracks.

Today Berlin remains the global capital of techno. Berghain is the reference point for everything from door policy to acoustic design to how long a set should be (often 5-8 hours). If you're in Berlin on a weekend and want to understand what techno really is, the answer is usually in Friedrichshain or Kreuzberg at 5am.
