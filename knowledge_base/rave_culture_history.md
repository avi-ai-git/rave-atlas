# Rave Culture and History

## Origins: Illegal Warehouse Raves

- Rave culture emerged from underground warehouse parties in the mid-to-late 1980s
- Illegal raves took place in abandoned industrial spaces, fields, and squats
- Influenced by Jamaican sound system culture brought to UK by Caribbean migrants
- Early raves were free or donation-based, community-organized, anti-commercial
- The term "rave" comes from 1960s mod/soul scene but was revived for acid house parties

## The Second Summer of Love (1988-1989)

- UK experienced a cultural explosion summer 1988 as acid house hit mainstream consciousness
- Massive illegal warehouse parties and field raves across Britain
- MDMA (ecstasy) culture merged with Chicago house and Detroit techno imports
- Sense of unity, peace, love, and togetherness - echoing 1960s hippie values but with electronic music
- Smiley face became the symbol of the movement
- Broke down social barriers - people from different classes, races, backgrounds dancing together

## The Criminal Justice and Public Order Act (1994)

- UK government's attempt to shut down rave culture
- Passed November 2, 1994, giving police power to "remove persons attending or preparing for a rave"
- Defined rave as gathering of 20+ people with music "wholly or predominantly characterized by the emission of a succession of repetitive beats"
- Gave police power to stop people traveling to raves within 5-mile radius
- Result: Rave culture went deeper underground and also moved into legal club venues
- Many see the Act as oppressive legislation that tried to criminalize youth culture
- Paradoxically strengthened the scene by making it more defiant and community-focused

## Warehouse Raves and Squat Culture

- Abandoned warehouses, factories, and industrial spaces became temples for electronic music
- Post-industrial urban decay (especially in cities like Detroit and Manchester) provided perfect venues
- Squatters would take over empty buildings for parties lasting days
- DIY ethos - ravers built their own soundsystems, organized their own events
- Minimal lighting, raw spaces, focus entirely on sound and collective experience
- After Berlin Wall fell (1989), abandoned East Berlin spaces became global center for warehouse raves

## Free Parties and Teknival Movement

- Free parties: Completely underground, donation-based or free events with no commercial element
- Teknival: Free festival movement, especially strong in France and UK
- Spiral Tribe and other sound systems organized massive free parties across Europe in 1990s
- Travelers and crusty-ravers (counterculture nomads) carried soundsystems between events
- Often clashed with police but maintained anti-commercial, anti-establishment ethos
- Teknival gatherings could last several days with multiple soundsystems
- Castlemorton Common Festival (May 1992, UK) drew 20,000-30,000 people and led to government crackdown

## Formats: How Parties Differ

### Club Night
- Licensed venue, door charge, resident DJs plus guests
- Set opening and closing times (though Berlin clubs may be open 48+ hours)
- Professional sound system, bar service, toilets, cloakroom
- Expected behavior and dress codes enforced by door policy

### Illegal Rave / Warehouse Party
- No license, location revealed last minute or by word of mouth
- DIY soundsystem, often no bar or facilities
- Risk of police shutdown, entry often by donation
- Raw, underground atmosphere

### Festival
- Multiple stages, days-long events, camping
- Commercial or boutique, licensed and legal
- Wide range of electronic music genres
- Examples: Tomorrowland, Awakenings, Junction 2

### Open-Air / Outdoor Party
- Outdoor venues, seasonal (summer primarily)
- Beach clubs, parks, gardens
- Daytime and nighttime vibes differ significantly
- Berlin's Tempelhof park spontaneous parties during COVID lockdowns

### After-Hours
- Starts when clubs close (6am-10am)
- For those not ready to stop
- Often more stripped-down, intimate
- Sunday daytime sessions in Berlin clubs are legendary

### Free Party
- No entry fee, no commercial element
- Often in remote outdoor locations
- DIY, anti-establishment
- Risk of police intervention

## Anatomy of a Night: Timing and Energy Flow

### Warm-Up (10pm-1am)
- Slower BPMs, groovy, setting the vibe
- Resident DJs often play warm-up
- Crowd arrives gradually, dancefloor not yet packed
- Locals know to arrive during this time to avoid tourist rush

### Build (1am-3am)
- Energy increases, BPMs rise
- Dancefloor fills, peak-time approaching
- DJs start introducing bigger tracks but still building tension

### Peak-Time (3am-6am)
- The core of the night, dancefloor at capacity
- Hardest, most intense music
- 140 BPM techno, big anthems, hands-in-the-air moments
- This is what Berghain's main floor is designed for
- Euphoria, collective experience, peak energy

### Afterhours (6am-12pm)
- Energy mellows but intensity can remain
- Sunrise sets, Panorama Bar upstairs opens in Berghain
- Groovy house, slower techno, more melodic
- Crowd thins but diehards remain
- Sunday daytime raving is Berlin specialty

### Why Locals Arrive at 2am
- Avoid tourist peak (midnight-2am)
- Peak-time is 3-6am when best DJs play
- Better crowd, more serious ravers
- Warm-up is for insiders who know

## The Roles: Who Does What

### Resident DJ
- Plays regularly at a specific club (e.g., weekly, monthly)
- Sets the club's musical identity and vibe
- Often plays warm-up or afterhours slots
- Deep knowledge of the crowd and space
- Ben Klock and Marcel Dettmann are Berghain residents
- Residents are the soul of a club - they are there every week, not just for payday

### Touring DJ / Headliner
- Travels internationally, plays peak-time slots
- Draws crowds based on name recognition
- May have less connection to venue than residents
- Higher booking fees

### Live Act
- Performs music with hardware (drum machines, synthesizers, controllers)
- Less predictable than DJs, more improvisation
- Artists like Surgeon, Jeff Mills, and Richie Hawtin known for live sets

### Promoter
- Organizes events, books DJs, handles logistics
- Can be commercial or underground collectives
- Club culture depends on quality promoters who care about music not just profit

## Soundsystem Culture

### Funktion-One: The Gold Standard
- UK-based loudspeaker manufacturer founded by Tony Andrews
- Renowned for clarity, low distortion, controlled directivity
- Berghain, Fabric (London), and Printworks use Funktion-One
- Audiophile club crowds specifically seek Funktion-One venues
- You feel the music physically - bass is clean and powerful, not muddy
- "The rig" is as important as the DJ for true heads

### DIY Soundsystems
- Free party culture built own massive soundsystems
- Spiral Tribe, Bedlam, and other collectives traveled with rigs
- Pride in self-built, self-maintained equipment
- Soundsystem = community identity

### Why Soundsystem Matters
- Electronic music is felt as much as heard
- Sub-bass frequencies you feel in your chest
- Poor sound ruins even the best DJ
- Club reputation tied to sound quality
- Berghain's acoustics are specifically designed for low-end frequency response

## The Social Contract: Unwritten Rules

### Respect the Space
- The dancefloor is sacred - treat it with reverence
- Give people room, don't crowd or shove
- If you bump into someone, apologize
- Look after people - if someone seems in distress, get help

### Phones and Photos
- Most serious clubs enforce strict no-photo policies
- Camera stickers placed over phone cameras at door
- Filming someone dancing without consent is a violation
- Phone on dancefloor marks you as tourist
- "Be present" - don't document, experience

### Consent Culture
- Ask before entering someone's personal space
- No staring, no filming, no touching without clear consent
- Queer and fetish parties have explicit consent rules
- The dancefloor is a safe space for self-expression - protect that

### Energy and Presence
- Bring positive energy, stay present
- Dance for yourself, not to be seen
- Collective experience - you are part of something bigger
- Respect the music and the people around you

### Know Your Limits
- Stay hydrated (but don't overhydrate - that can also be dangerous)
- Take breaks, use the chill-out room
- Watch for signs of overheating or dehydration in yourself and others
- If you feel unwell, ask for help or find club staff

## Rave Culture as Social Movement

- Electronic music created spaces for marginalized communities - LGBTQ+, POC, working class
- Rejection of mainstream commercialism and corporate culture
- Anti-hierarchical - everyone equal on the dancefloor
- Politically engaged - free parties were acts of reclaiming public space
- Berlin's techno scene tied to post-reunification freedom and squatter movements
- Rave culture challenged laws, police, and establishment control of public space
- Connection to earlier countercultures (hippies, punks) but with its own identity
