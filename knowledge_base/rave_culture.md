# Rave Culture — What to Know Before You Go

## What Is a Rave

A rave is an electronic music event, usually held at night and running until the early morning or beyond — sometimes for 12, 24, or even 72 hours. The music is the point. Not the bar, not the crowd-watching, not the Instagram moment. The floor is a shared space where people go to lose themselves in rhythm together. That's the original principle, and in Berlin it's still mostly true.

The term "rave" covers a huge range: illegal warehouse parties in the 90s, licensed clubs like Berghain, open-air festivals, and intimate basement events with 80 people. What they share is the primacy of sound, darkness, and sustained duration.

## Berlin Raves Specifically

Berlin raves operate differently from most cities. Some things that are unusual to outsiders:

- **Events start late and end very late.** A Berlin club night starts at midnight or 1am and often runs until Sunday evening or Monday morning. Arriving at 11pm is considered early. 2am is a sensible time to arrive. 4am is not unusual.
- **There is no opening or closing act in the traditional sense.** DJs play long sets — 4, 6, 8 hours. The music shifts gradually rather than with dramatic changeovers. You're meant to arrive into the music, not wait for it.
- **The dark room is standard.** Most Berlin clubs have almost no ambient light on the dance floor. This is intentional: it removes self-consciousness, focuses attention on sound, and equalises the crowd. If you can't handle near-total darkness, this is worth knowing in advance.
- **The door is a real filter.** At Berghain and a few other venues, the door staff (Türsteher) can and do turn people away based on how they carry themselves. It is not about looks or dress in the conventional sense. It's about whether you seem like you're there for the music. Dressing simply and dark helps. Going in small groups (2-4 people) is easier than large ones. If you get turned away, you get turned away — it happens to everyone eventually.

## What to Expect Inside

- **Sound systems designed for sustained exposure.** Modern techno clubs run their systems at high volumes but often with extreme clarity — d&b Audiotechnik or similar. The bass is physical. Earplugs are strongly recommended for any set over 2-3 hours.
- **A coat check (Garderobe).** You will almost always leave your coat, bag, and anything you don't need on the floor. The coat check is part of the ritual. Most charge €2-3.
- **Cash at the bar.** Many Berlin clubs are cash-only or cash-preferred. Bring enough before you go in.
- **No cameras on the floor.** The Berghain no-photography rule (the sticker over the phone camera) has spread widely through Berlin clubs. Respect it. Taking photos on the floor marks you as someone who doesn't understand the culture. Checking your phone constantly has the same effect.
- **Water.** Good clubs have free or cheap water at the bar. If dancing hard, you need to drink regularly. Dehydration combined with heat and a loud soundsystem is genuinely dangerous.

## Etiquette

- **Don't talk through sets.** If you want to have a conversation, move away from the speakers. The dance floor is for dancing.
- **Respect people's space.** The floor is communal but not a place to grab or approach people uninvited. This is particularly true at queer and experimental spaces like KitKat or Tresor.
- **Don't interrupt a moment.** If the DJ is building to something and the room is locked in, don't push through, don't shout to your friend, don't check your phone. Read the room.
- **Dress practically.** You will sweat. Layers work better than one heavy jacket. Comfortable shoes are more important than stylish ones — you may be on your feet for 6 hours.
- **Queuing.** Don't push, don't send people ahead to hold your spot, don't argue with the door staff. Patience is part of the experience.

## What Not to Do

- Do not bring large groups and try to keep them together on the dance floor.
- Do not stand in the middle of the floor and look at your phone for an extended period.
- Do not request songs — this is not a wedding DJ.
- Do not try to film the DJ booth unless you've been explicitly told it's allowed.
- Do not make racist, sexist, or homophobic comments or gestures. Berlin's club culture is explicitly queer-friendly and anti-fascist. You will be ejected and should be.
- Do not arrive drunk. Many door staff will turn away people who are clearly drunk, especially at places like Berghain. Arriving in a good headspace — alert, settled, ready — is the goal.

## The Queer Club Scene

Berlin's electronic music scene has deep roots in queer culture. Berghain started as a gay club and still has a queer Sunday party (Lab.Oratory). KitKat Club is a sex-positive venue where dress codes on certain nights are explicitly body-positive. Many of Berlin's most important nights are queer-coded or explicitly LGBTQ+ spaces.

If you're attending one of these nights, read the event's own guidelines before you arrive. Respect the space and the people who built it.

## After the Night

The comedown after a long night is real. Darkness, loud music, and sustained physical activity all affect your nervous system. Expect to feel tired and possibly emotionally tender for a day or two after a heavy night. This is normal. Sleep, food, and rest are the recovery.
