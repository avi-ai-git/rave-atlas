# How an Electronic Dance Music Track is Structured

## Why structure matters

Electronic dance music is designed for DJs. Unlike a pop song written to be heard from start to finish on the radio, a techno or house track is a tool: it needs a long intro to mix in over, a clear peak to exploit, and a long outro to mix out from. Understanding this structure helps you understand why tracks are the length they are, and why the DJ's job is to manage energy over hours rather than play individual songs.

## The functional sections

### Intro
**Purpose:** Give the outgoing DJ time to beat-match and blend this track in over the one currently playing.
**Contents:** Usually just the kick drum, hi-hat, and maybe a bass pulse. Stripped of almost everything that makes the track interesting.
**Length:** 32-64 bars (about 1-2 minutes at 130 BPM). Techno intros tend to be longer than house intros.

### Build
**Purpose:** Introduce the track's main elements gradually, raising energy and expectation.
**Contents:** Bass line enters, then pads or chords, then percussive elements. Filters open up.
**Length:** 16-32 bars.

### Main body / drop
**Purpose:** The full groove, everything in. This is what the track is "about."
**Contents:** All elements present: kick, bass, hi-hat, clap, synth or pad, any vocal or sample.
**Length:** 32-96 bars (1-3 minutes). May repeat with variations or breakdowns.

### Breakdown
**Purpose:** Release tension by temporarily removing the kick. Creates an emotional moment and anticipation for the return.
**Contents:** Kick drops out; melody, pad, or atmosphere is exposed. Sometimes the bass drops too.
**Length:** 8-16 bars. Not all tracks have a breakdown; minimal and industrial techno often skip it.
**Note for DJs:** Breakdowns are both an opportunity (to mix another track in on the atmospheric section) and a risk (you can lose the crowd if you hold too long). Experienced DJs use breakdowns deliberately.

### Outro
**Purpose:** Give the incoming DJ time to mix the next track in.
**Contents:** Elements drop away symmetrically with the intro, you end up with just kick and hi-hat again.
**Length:** 32-64 bars. Mirrors the intro length.

## Energy arc and DJ use

A single track's internal energy arc (build → peak → breakdown → peak → outro) is the micro-level. A DJ set is the macro-level: a 5-hour set is built from hundreds of these micro-arcs, chained together so the overall energy of the night rises, peaks (typically 2-4am), and gradually descends.

DJs manipulate energy by:
- **Track selection:** Choosing tracks with higher or lower energy levels
- **Mix points:** Mixing at the peak vs in the intro/outro changes how much energy is "donated" to the transition
- **Looping:** Holding an element longer to sustain a peak
- **Filtering:** Using EQ to remove bass or highs during the mix, then restoring them for impact

## Layers in a typical techno or house track

| Layer | Role | Typical sounds |
|---|---|---|
| Kick | Pulse, anchor | 808/909 kick, punchy or round |
| Sub bass | Low-end weight, groove | Sine wave, 303 bass, synthesised sub |
| Hi-hat | Forward momentum | Closed/open hats, shuffled patterns |
| Clap / snare | Backbeat, texture | 909 snare, hand clap samples |
| Pad / chords | Atmosphere, harmonic content | Synth pads, organ, string samples |
| Lead synth | Melodic identity | Arpeggio, hook, acid line |
| Percussion | Texture, complexity | Shakers, rimshots, congas, metallic hits |
| Vocal / sample | Human element, emotion | Vocal chops, speech samples, full vocals |
| FX | Transitions, surprise | White noise sweeps, reverb tails, risers |

Not all tracks use all layers. Minimal techno may use only kick, hi-hat, bass, and a single evolving synth element. The restraint is intentional.

## The Roland TR-909 and TB-303

Two machines define the sound palette of electronic dance music:

**Roland TR-909 (1983):** A drum machine initially considered a failure (it didn't sound like real drums). Its kick drum, snare, hi-hat, and clap became the template for techno and house. The kick is punchy and subby; the hi-hat has a characteristic metallic decay; the snare is crisp and snappy. Every major DAW has a 909 emulation. The original hardware sells for thousands.

**Roland TB-303 (1981):** A bass synthesiser with a sequencer that was impossible to program intuitively. Musicians abandoned it. DJs and producers in Chicago found them in second-hand shops, discovered that "wrong" programming produced squelching, resonant, unstable bass lines, and acid house was born. The 303's characteristic sound (filter cutoff sweeping open, resonance cranked, notes gliding between pitches) is the most recognised sound in electronic music.
