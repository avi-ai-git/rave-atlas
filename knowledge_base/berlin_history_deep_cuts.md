# Berlin Club History: Deep Cuts

## The pre-Tresor period (1988 to 1991)

### Ufo Club (1988 to 1990)
- The first significant techno and acid house venue in Berlin, located in a basement on Jerusalemer Strasse in Kreuzberg.
- Founded and run by DJs Wolle XDP (Wolfgang Lamprecht) and Jonnie Stieler, among others.
- One of the first places in Berlin where house and techno from Chicago, Detroit and the UK Second Summer of Love was played regularly. Very small, DIY, and illegal in spirit.
- Closed in 1990 when the building was sold.

### Tekknozid (1990)
- The series of illegal techno raves in the months immediately after the Wall fell (November 1989).
- Organised by Wolle XDP, Jonnie Stieler and others from the Ufo circle; effectively the mobile continuation of Ufo culture into the new East Berlin spaces.
- Tekknozid events were held in abandoned East Berlin industrial buildings in 1990, six to twelve months before Tresor opened. They are documented in "Der Klang der Familie" (2012) by Felix Denk and Sven von Thulen, the essential oral history of Berlin techno.
- These raves established the template for the early 1990s Berlin underground: abandoned space, hard techno, no decor, darkness.

### "Der Klang der Familie" (The Sound of the Family)
- The canonical oral history of early Berlin techno, published in German in 2012 by Suhrkamp Verlag (authored by Felix Denk and Sven von Thulen). Essential source for the Tekknozid era through the mid-1990s.
- Covers: Ufo, Tekknozid, the opening of Tresor, the Love Parade origins, the Berlin-Detroit connection, and the first decade of the scene in first-person testimony.

## Hard Wax record shop
- Opened 1989 in Kreuzberg by Mark Ernestus (one half of Basic Channel), originally with Moritz von Oswald. At Skalitzer Strasse 8a (later Paul-Lincke-Ufer 44a, still the address as of 2025).
- A listen-before-you-buy specialist shop for house, techno and dub. No mainstream stock; everything selected.
- The spiritual home of the Basic Channel / Chain Reaction / Maurizio dub techno sound: deep, dark, dubby, built for listening.
- Basic Channel (Ernestus and von Oswald) defined dub techno: sparse, echoing chords, near-static rhythm, long-form and hypnotic. Reference releases include BCM-02, BCM-04 (Basic Channel), and the Chain Reaction label series (Maurizio, Enforcement, Vainqueur, Porter Ricks).
- Hard Wax was the institution where half of Berlin's DJs bought records for two decades. It seeded the taste that shaped the city's sound.
- Still open and operating as a specialist store and label distributor.

## Tresor origin (1991)
- Founded March 1991 by Dimitri Hegemann in the vault of the former Wertheim department store at Potsdamer Platz (the specific address was Leipziger Strasse corner; the vault was the safe room of the old GDR-era Wertheim successor store).
- Hegemann had run the Ufo Club and had been making contact with Detroit producers through Ron Trent, Juan Atkins and others. Tresor opened with a mission: connecting Berlin's vacant East with Detroit's abandoned factories through the same techno music.
- First Tresor Records release was the X-101 project from Underground Resistance (Jeff Mills, Mike Banks, Robert Hood, released 1991). The label's catalogue became the definitive document of the Berlin-Detroit axis.
- Closed the original vault site 16 April 2005 when the Potsdamer Platz development reclaimed it. Reopened 24 May 2007 in the decommissioned coal-fired heating power plant on Koepenicker Strasse (now called Kraftwerk Berlin).
- Hegemann was awarded the Berlin Order of Merit (Verdienstorden des Landes Berlin) in April 2025 for his contributions to techno and club culture.

## Ostgut (1998 to 2003): the direct Berghain predecessor
- A gay fetish and techno club in a large industrial building on the grounds of the former East German freight railroad depot (Ostgutgelande) in Friedrichshain.
- Founded and run by Norbert Thormann and Michael Teufele (who later opened Berghain at a nearby location).
- Hosted the early Snax fetish parties (see berlin_party_series.md) and established the queer, sex-positive techno foundation that Berghain inherited.
- Closed 2003 when the lease ended. The Ostgut team searched for a new venue and found the former Heizkraftwerk at Am Wriezener Bahnhof, which they converted and opened as Berghain in 2004.
- The thread from Ufo to Tekknozid to Tresor to Ostgut to Berghain is a direct historical line.

## Love Parade (1989 to 2010)
- Started as a small political demonstration on 1 July 1989 in West Berlin by DJ Dr. Motte (Matthias Roeingh) and approximately 150 people (some sources say 200), marching from Kurfurstendamm to Tiergarten with a flatbed truck and a basic soundsystem.
- The stated goal was peace and international understanding through music; the original application to the Berlin authorities was filed as a political demonstration rather than a commercial event.
- Grew to roughly 500,000 in 1995 (first year in reunified central Berlin), and peaked at an estimated 1.5 million attendees in 1999.
- Moved to other German cities from 2006 (Essen 2007, Dortmund 2008) after Berlin withdrew public funding.
- Ended with the Duisburg disaster on 24 July 2010: 21 people were killed and over 500 injured in a crowd crush at the tunnel entrance to the Duisburg event. Dr. Motte had publicly opposed the Duisburg move as unsafe.
- Dr. Motte (Matthias Roeingh) later founded Rave the Planet (with Ellen Dosch-Roeingh), which campaigns to protect club culture and successfully lobbied for the UNESCO national heritage inscription in 2024.

## Bar25 and the Holzmarkt / Kater lineage
- Bar25 opened in 2003 at Holzmarktstrasse 25, Friedrichshain, on a disused riverside strip between Ostbahnhof and Jannowitzbrucke. It was a camp, bohemian beach bar and small club on the Spree: hammocks, fairy lights, wooden huts, a resident cat, a sauna, outdoor dancefloor.
- Widely celebrated for its inclusive, hippie-circus culture; it attracted an arts crowd and DJs alongside the techno heads.
- Forcibly closed in 2010 when the site's lease expired. The closure was resisted and became a symbol of Berlin's gentrification battle.
- The Bar25 team (including Christoph Klenzendorf) regrouped and opened Kater Holzig (2011 to 2014) in a soap factory in Kreuzberg, then Kater Blau (2014, Holzmarktstrasse 25, the original site) as part of the Holzmarkt cooperative urban project.
- Holzmarkt 25 is a deliberately countercultural development: a cooperative that bought the site to prevent luxury development, building a permanent cultural quarter with Kater Blau, the Pampa restaurant, a mediahouse and a cultural association. Kater Blau was renamed Kater Club in August 2025.
- The lineage is Bar25 (2003 to 2010) to Kater Holzig (2011 to 2014) to Kater Blau / Kater Club (2014 onward).

## Griessmuhle and the Revier Sudost story
- A former grain mill on the Neukolln Canal (Sonnenallee 221), opened as a club around 2011. Co-founded by David Ciura and the team that would become the RSO founders.
- Hosted Cocktail d'Amore (the marathon sex-positive queer techno series), a record shop, flea markets, a canteen, and table tennis.
- Evicted February 2020 when the building was sold to developers. The final Cocktail d'Amore marathon ran 31 January to 3 February 2020. The closure became a symbol of Clubsterben.
- The team relocated to the Barenquell brewery complex in Schoneweide, transforming it into RSO.Berlin, which opened in 2021.

## The A100 autobahn threat
- Berlin's Senate has approved the continued construction of the A100 urban motorway through Friedrichshain and Neukolln. The planned 17th construction phase would run directly through or adjacent to about blank, Else, Renate, and other club spaces.
- The Berlin club community has been fighting this for over a decade. It is a live threat as of 2025 with no resolution.

## The economic weight of club culture

- The Clubcommission (Berlin's club industry association) estimated in their 2018 report that club tourism generates approximately 3 million visitors per year specifically for nightlife, contributing around 1.48 billion euros to the Berlin economy annually.
- This figure is cited in policy debates to argue that clubs deserve the same municipal protection as theatres, concert halls and museums.
- The economic case was strengthened by Germany's recognition of Berlin techno as intangible cultural heritage in March 2024, and by the Finanzgericht Berlin-Brandenburg VAT ruling (case 5 K 5089/14) which classified Berghain admission as a cultural service exempt from standard entertainment tax, a first for a nightclub in Germany.
