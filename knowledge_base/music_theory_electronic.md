# Harmonic Analysis for Electronic Music

## Why Theory Matters in Electronic Music

Electronic music has a reputation for being post-harmonic, pure rhythm, pure texture. That is partly true and partly wrong. Techno at its most abstract (Drexciyan electro, early Surgeon, late Regis) genuinely operates outside tonal harmony. But almost all house music, a significant proportion of techno, and virtually all trance, deep house, and melodic techno depend on harmonic structures borrowed from jazz, classical, and funk, deployed with electronic tools.

Knowing the harmonic language helps you hear the difference between a Kerri Chandler bassline that is *working* the chord and a loop that just sits on one note. It helps you understand why one drop lands and another doesn't. And it helps you write music that makes structural sense rather than sounding like it was assembled randomly.

## Scales and Modes Most Used in Electronic Music

### Minor scales (the workhorse)

Most techno and house operates in a **natural minor** (Aeolian) tonality or its close relative the **Dorian mode**. The reason is practical: minor keys produce tension without requiring resolution in the way that Western common-practice harmony does. You can sit on a minor chord for four minutes and it does not demand anything from you.

- **Natural minor (Aeolian)**: 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7. The default. Dark, inward, stable in its instability.
- **Dorian mode**: 1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 ♭7. Natural minor with a raised 6th degree. Sounds slightly warmer, more ambiguous between minor and minor-with-major-colour. Most commonly heard in deep house and classic Chicago house, the Dorian raised 6th gives a soulful, bittersweet quality.
- **Phrygian mode**: 1 ♭2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7. Natural minor with a flattened 2nd. Darker and more Spanish or Middle Eastern in flavour. Used in dark techno, industrial techno, and EBM. The ♭2 above the root creates strong gravitational pull downward, good for ominous, descending basslines.

### The Phrygian dominant

The **Phrygian dominant** scale (1 ♭2 3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7) is the fifth mode of the harmonic minor, it has the dark flatted second of Phrygian but a *major* third. This is the scale you hear in flamenco, in film noir, and in techno that wants to sound threatening or ritualistic without being obviously minor. DVS1, Dax J, and some of Alignment's harder material operates here.

### Major and Lydian (less common, more specific)

Major keys in techno are rare, they read as triumphant or anthemic, which runs counter to the aesthetic. But in:
- **Disco-rooted house**: major or mixolydian (major with a flatted 7th) is the default. Frankie Knuckles, Larry Heard's more optimistic material, Kerri Chandler's New Jersey gospel influence.
- **Trance**: major keys are standard. Uplifting trance specifically requires major resolution, the drop lands on a major chord.
- **Melodic techno / Afterlife sound**: **Lydian mode** (major with a raised 4th degree) appears often. The raised 4th creates a dreamlike, floating quality, unresolved in a pleasant way, which fits the aesthetic of Massano, Tale Of Us, Anyma. It sounds "cinematic" because film composers use it for exactly the same reason.

## Chord Structures: How House Builds Harmony

House music inherited its harmonic vocabulary directly from gospel, soul, and jazz. Understanding that inheritance explains why it sounds the way it does.

### The ii, V, I in house

The most fundamental jazz progression, **ii, V, I**, shows up everywhere in deep house:
- In A minor: Bm7(♭5) → E7 → Am7
- The tension/release dynamic is the point: ii creates momentum, V demands resolution, I provides it.

Larry Heard's "Can You Feel It" essentially loops a version of this. Chicago house piano tracks from the late 1980s are built almost entirely on ii, V, I and its relatives.

### The minor ii, V, I (most common in deep house)

In practice, house more often uses **minor ii, V, I** or the **turnaround without full resolution**:
- Am → Dm → E (or E7) → Am (four-bar loop)
- This cycle can be compressed, extended, or have the resolution deferred, the last bar resolving "later" is a standard tool for building and releasing tension.

### The "soulful chord": minor 7ths and 9ths

Deep house uses **extended chords** rather than triads:
- A **minor 7th** (root + minor 3rd + 5th + minor 7th) sounds richer and less resolved than a plain minor triad.
- Adding the **9th** (same as a major 2nd but an octave higher) adds a sense of yearning or sophistication.
- House piano chords are almost always minor 7ths or dominant 9ths, the extensions are what give them the "soulful" quality.

### Suspended chords (sus2 and sus4)

The **sus4** and **sus2**, chords that replace the 3rd with a 4th or 2nd, appear constantly in house and some techno. They sound unresolved but not tense in a harsh way. The missing 3rd means you cannot tell whether the chord is major or minor. This is exactly why they work in electronic music: they are tonally ambiguous, which allows them to loop without creating the demand for resolution that a fully voiced triad would.

The four-on-the-floor kick and the loop structure of dance music work against classical harmonic resolution. Sus chords are the workaround, they provide harmonic interest without harmonic obligation.

## Tension and Release: The Core Structural Principle

All functional harmony is about creating tension and releasing it. In electronic music, this operates at multiple timescales simultaneously:

### Harmonic tension (within a loop or phrase)
- A dominant seventh chord (1 3 5 ♭7) wants to resolve to its tonic. If the chord loop keeps cycling back to the dominant, it keeps deferring resolution.
- The simplest tension tool: hold the fifth in the bass and move the upper voices. The bass note creates stability; the moving chords create forward momentum.

### Textural tension (arrangement-level)
- Removing elements from a dense arrangement creates tension through absence, the mix suddenly feels "empty," which the brain reads as unresolved.
- Classic techno and house technique: strip the bassline out for 8 bars, leave only kick and hi-hat, then bring everything back. The return IS the release.
- The "filter sweep" (gradually closing a low-pass filter before a drop) is a textural approximation of harmonic tension, it removes overtones, making the sound feel suppressed, and the reopening of the filter is physically satisfying.

### Rhythmic tension (syncopation and polyrhythm)
- A 4/4 bassline with a syncopated attack on the "and" of beats creates tension against the four-on-the-floor kick. The bassline is "fighting" the kick.
- Resolving the syncopation back to on-beat attacks at a drop is a release.
- Polyrhythmic patterns (3-against-4, 5-against-4) in hi-hats or percussion layers create constant micro-tension.

## The Riff vs. the Chord: Techno's Approach

Techno, especially Berlin techno, largely abandoned conventional chord progressions in favour of **riff-based tonality**:
- A single repeated motif, a bass stab, a high synth hook, a one-bar acid bassline, establishes the tonal centre without harmonic movement.
- Tension comes from **timbre, filter, modulation, and texture** rather than from chord changes.
- A TB-303 pattern looping on a single note is not "harmonically static" in the way classical theory would label it, the resonance, cutoff frequency, and envelope decay create a continuous micro-dynamic that feels like movement even with no chord change.

This is why calling Berlin techno "monotonous" misunderstands the aesthetics. The harmonic movement is happening in the timbre, not in the chords. Ostgut Ton and Klockworks records are studies in how much expressive range exists within a tonal centre.

## Specific Examples: Genre-by-Genre

### Deep house
- Key signatures: typically minor, often Dorian. A minor, D minor, E minor.
- Typical chord vocabulary: minor 7ths, dominant 9ths, sus4, ii, V, I turnarounds.
- Typical structure: 8-bar chord loop with constant variation in voicing, velocity, and timing. Piano chords are often "comped" rather than stabbed, rolled or arpeggiated, borrowing the jazz vocabulary.

### Techno (hypnotic / Berlin style)
- Harmony: single tonal centre or no clear tonal centre. Phrygian or Phrygian dominant for dark flavours; Aeolian for more emotional material.
- No chord changes in most peak-time material. Tension is timbral.
- Key tool: the drone, a sustained low note that creates tonal anchor; all other elements orbit around it.

### Trance
- Key signatures: almost always major. A major, E major, B major.
- Structure: clear 8-bar and 16-bar cycles, explicit build-up → breakdown → drop.
- The "breakdown": the melody (usually a sustained pad or lead synth line) is exposed over stripped-back percussion. The full re-entry of the kick and bassline IS the harmonic and rhythmic resolution simultaneously.
- Typical progression in classic trance: I → V → vi → IV (the pop-trance loop). Uplifting trance builds to a dominant (V) and drops back to the tonic (I), textbook tension and release.

### Melodic techno / Afterlife sound
- Hybrid: techno rhythmic grid (120-126 BPM, four-on-the-floor) with house or neo-classical harmonic vocabulary.
- Extended chords, suspended chords, and Lydian tonality appear frequently.
- Contrast is the key structural move: stripped minimal opening section with a single element; gradual harmonic and timbral build; a release that feels earned because of the length of the build.

### EBM and industrial techno
- Harmony: Phrygian or chromatic. Parallel minor chords moving by semitones.
- Rhythm: often syncopated against the kick, EBM patterns are built on snare placement off the beat.
- The riff is primary; chords secondary or absent.

## BPM, Time Signature, and Harmonic Expectations

- **120-124 BPM (house)**: slower tempo leaves room for harmonic elaboration, each beat has physical space, and chords have time to register. Extended jazz-influenced voicings work well.
- **128-135 BPM (techno)**: at this tempo, complex chords can sound cluttered. Simpler voicings, single stabs, bass notes, a sustained pad, work better. The kick is dense enough that the harmony must be spacious.
- **138-145 BPM (trance)**: fast enough that whole-bar chords sustain through a lot of kicks. The speed creates propulsion; the harmony provides the emotional arc.
- **150-160 BPM (drum & bass, jungle)**: at this tempo the harmonic content is almost always in a pad or background layer, individual chords cannot register at this speed in the foreground.

## Practical: Recognising Key and Mode by Ear

1. Find the tonal centre: what note does the bassline resolve to, or circle around? That is probably the root.
2. Is the third above that note major or minor? (Major 3rd = brighter, hopeful. Minor 3rd = darker, inward.)
3. What is the second scale degree? If it is a semitone above the root (♭2), you are probably in Phrygian.
4. Does the sixth feel natural? In Dorian, the sixth is raised one semitone compared to natural minor, it gives Dorian its warmer, slightly jazz-flavoured quality.
5. Is there any clear harmonic movement (chord changes), or does the track sit on one tonal centre and move in timbre? If the latter, look for the riff structure rather than a progression.
